\section{What problem is ShapeMix-ATAC solving?}

Spatial ATAC-seq measures open chromatin in a tissue section.  Its fragments file records deduplicated DNA fragments assigned to spatial barcodes.  Each fragment provides two Tn5 cut sites, and a spot may contain more than one nucleus.  Therefore, the observed signal is a mixture.

\begin{bio}
Think of a spot as a small square drawn on top of tissue.  Inside that square, there may be T cells, B cells, fibroblasts, tumor cells, neurons, or other cell types.  ATAC-seq does not directly say which nucleus produced each fragment or cut site.  ShapeMix-ATAC tries to infer the hidden cell mixture from the cut-site counts and their parent fragments' lengths.
\end{bio}

The ordinary deconvolution question is:
\[
\text{observed mixed signal} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \text{cell-type amounts in the spot}.
\]

The ShapeMix-ATAC question is richer:
\[
\text{observed peak totals and parent-fragment length composition}
\quad \Longrightarrow \quad
\text{cell-type amounts with uncertainty}.
\]

\subsection{Why ATAC needs a special model}

Spatial transcriptomics mostly counts RNA molecules from genes.  Spatial ATAC counts DNA fragments from open chromatin peaks.  ATAC fragments also have physical features:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
    \item \textbf{Fragment length:} short fragments often come from nucleosome-free DNA; longer fragments can span nucleosomes.
    \item \textbf{Position inside a peak:} fragments may concentrate near the center, left edge, right edge, or flanking regions.
    \item \textbf{Motif-centered pattern:} cuts around a transcription-factor motif can carry footprint-like information.
\end{itemize}

\begin{idea}
The primary ShapeMix data object is a three-dimensional count table:
\[
\boxed{Y_{s,p,b} = \text{deduplicated Tn5 cut sites in spot }s,\text{ peak }p,\text{ parent-length bin }b.}
\]
Each cut site inherits the length bin of its parent fragment.  A usual Cell Ranger-compatible peak-count model uses only \(N_{s,p}=\sum_b Y_{s,p,b}\).  ShapeMix keeps the extra index \(b\).  A unique-fragment-count analysis would be a separately versioned sensitivity analysis with its own matched baseline.
\end{idea}

The primary implementation uses exactly three parent-fragment length bins:
\[
 b=1: [0,100), \qquad b=2: [100,250), \qquad b=3: [250,\infty)\ \text{bp}.
\]
Length is \(\texttt{chromEnd}-\texttt{chromStart}\) from the adjusted, zero-based half-open 10x fragment interval.  Each fragments-file row is already a deduplicated fragment; the \texttt{readSupport} field is ignored in the primary analysis so PCR read-pair support is not counted again.  Both cut sites inherit the same parent-length bin and are assigned independently to the unique selected, non-overlapping peak containing them.  The right-cut coordinate convention must be validated by reconstructing selected columns of the official peak matrix before canonical data are frozen.

\section{A short Bayesian warm-up}

Bayesian modeling means we write down a story for how the data could have been generated.  Then we run the story backward.

\begin{mathbox}{Bayes rule in words}
\[
\text{posterior} \; \propto \; \text{likelihood} \times \text{prior}.
\]
In ShapeMix-ATAC:
\[
 p(\text{hidden cell mixture} \given \text{observed ATAC cut-site tensor})
 \propto
 p(\text{observed ATAC cut-site tensor} \given \text{hidden cell mixture})
 p(\text{hidden cell mixture}).
\]
\end{mathbox}

\subsection{Distribution cheat sheet}

\toprule
Distribution & Used for & Intuition \\
\midrule
Poisson & simple counts & Counts fluctuate around their mean. \\
Negative binomial & noisy sequencing counts & Like Poisson, but allows extra noise. \\
Gamma & positive amounts & Good for abundances, background, dispersion, and rates. \\
Log-normal & positive multiplicative effects & Good for depth/yield/bias factors. \\
Beta & probabilities between 0 and 1 & Good for peak accessibility probabilities. \\
Dirichlet & vectors that sum to 1 & Good for proportions, such as shape-bin probabilities. \\
Multinomial & splitting a fixed peak total among bins & Primary conditional likelihood for parent-length composition. \\
Normal / CAR normal & real-valued latent spatial fields & Good for smooth spatial variation. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\section{Start with the ordinary peak-count model}

Before adding shape, an ATAC peak-count model says:
\[
Y_{s,p} \sim \NB(\lambda_{s,p},\phi_p),
\]
where
\[
\lambda_{s,p}
= \ell_s\left[\beta_p + \sum_{c=1}^C z_{s,c} q_c d_p \theta_{c,p}\right].
\]

Read this as a sentence:

\begin{quote}
The expected ATAC fragments at peak \(p\) in spot \(s\) equal spot depth \(\ell_s\), times background plus the summed contributions from each cell type.
\end{quote}

The problem is that \(Y_{s,p}\) ignores the shapes of fragments inside the peak.

\section{The ShapeMix-ATAC likelihood}

\subsection{The core equation}

ShapeMix adds the shape-bin index \(b\):

\begin{mathbox}{Core ShapeMix likelihood}
\begin{align}
Y_{s,p,b} &\sim \NB(\lambda_{s,p,b},\phi_p), \label{eq:likelihood}\\[0.3em]
\lambda_{s,p,b}
&= \ell_s\left[\beta_{p,b} + \sum_{c=1}^{C} z_{s,c}\,q_c\,d_p\,\theta_{c,p}\,\omega_{c,p,b}\right]. \label{eq:lambda}
\end{align}
\end{mathbox}

This is the central model.  The new ShapeMix term is
\[
\boxed{\omega_{c,p,b}},
\]
which means:

\begin{quote}
Among fragments from cell type \(c\) at peak \(p\), what fraction should fall into shape bin \(b\)?
\end{quote}

\subsection{Meaning of each factor in \texorpdfstring{\(\lambda_{s,p,b}\)}{lambda}}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{L{0.18\linewidth}X}
\toprule
Term & Meaning \\
\midrule
\(\ell_s\) & sequencing-depth offset for spot \(s\).  A deeper spot produces more fragments overall. \\
\(\beta_{p,b}\) & background fragments for peak \(p\), shape bin \(b\), not explained by known cell types. \\
\(z_{s,c}\) & effective number of cells/nuclei of type \(c\) in spot \(s\).  This is the main hidden quantity. \\
\(q_c\) & per-cell ATAC yield for cell type \(c\).  Some cell types produce more accessible fragments per nucleus. \\
\(d_p\) & peak detectability.  Some peaks are easier to observe because of width, GC content, mappability, or Tn5 bias. \\
\(\theta_{c,p}\) & accessibility of peak \(p\) in cell type \(c\). \\
\(\omega_{c,p,b}\) & shape distribution: fraction of fragments from cell type \(c\), peak \(p\), assigned to bin \(b\). \\
\(\phi_p\) & negative-binomial dispersion for peak \(p\).  Smaller \(\phi_p\) means noisier counts. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\subsection{Negative-binomial parameterization}

This document uses a negative binomial with mean \(\lambda\) and dispersion \(\phi\):
\[
Y \sim \NB(\lambda,\phi)
\]
means
\[
\E[Y]=\lambda,
\qquad
\Var(Y)=\lambda+\frac{\lambda^2}{\phi}.
\]

A large \(\phi\) makes the model close to Poisson.  A small \(\phi\) allows extra sequencing noise.

The model can be taught as a recipe:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
    \item Learn each cell type's peak accessibility \(\theta_{c,p}\) from a scATAC or multiome reference.
    \item Learn each cell type's fragment-shape pattern \(\omega_{c,p,b}\) from the same reference.
    \item Guess a possible hidden cell mixture \(z_{s,c}\) for each spot.
    \item Use Equation~\eqref{eq:lambda} to predict expected counts \(\lambda_{s,p,b}\).
    \item Compare predicted counts with observed counts \(Y_{s,p,b}\).
    \item Update the hidden mixture and other uncertain parameters until the posterior is high.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Reference signatures}

The model needs a reference containing cells with known cell-type labels.  A single-cell multiome reference is especially useful because RNA can help label cell types while ATAC provides chromatin signatures.

\subsection{Peak accessibility prior}

Let \(R_{c,p}^{\mathrm{open}}\) and \(R_{c,p}^{\mathrm{closed}}\) be reference pseudo-counts for how often peak \(p\) appears open or closed in cell type \(c\).  Then:

\begin{mathbox}{Accessibility prior}
\begin{align}
\theta_{c,p} &\sim \Betadist\left(a_{\theta}+R_{c,p}^{\mathrm{open}},\; b_{\theta}+R_{c,p}^{\mathrm{closed}}\right).
\end{align}
\end{mathbox}

Here \(\theta_{c,p}\in[0,1]\).  Large \(\theta_{c,p}\) means peak \(p\) is often accessible in cell type \(c\).

\subsection{Shape prior}

For each cell type \(c\) and peak \(p\), the shape probabilities must sum to 1:
\[
\sum_{b=1}^{B}\omega_{c,p,b}=1,
\qquad
\omega_{c,p,b}\ge 0.
\]

A Dirichlet prior is natural:

\begin{mathbox}{Shape prior}
\begin{align}
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{c,p,\cdot}
&= (\omega_{c,p,1},\ldots,\omega_{c,p,B}),\\
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{c,p,\cdot}
&\sim \Dir\left(a_{\omega}\,\boldsymbol{u}_{\omega}+\boldsymbol{R}^{\mathrm{shape}}_{c,p,\cdot}\right).
\end{align}
\end{mathbox}

\(\boldsymbol{R}^{\mathrm{shape}}_{c,p,\cdot}\) is the vector of reference shape-bin counts.  \(\boldsymbol{u}_{\omega}\) is a default shape vector, such as uniform \((1/B,\ldots,1/B)\).

\begin{bio}
If a T cell reference says that fragments at peak \(p\) are usually short, then \(\omega_{T,p,\mathrm{short}}\) should be high.  If a B cell reference says the same peak mostly has mononucleosomal fragments, then \(\omega_{B,p,\mathrm{mono}}\) should be high.  ShapeMix uses this difference as extra evidence.
\end{bio}

\section{Priors for hidden cell abundance}

The main hidden quantity is \(z_{s,c}\), the effective amount of cell type \(c\) in spot \(s\).  It is positive:
\[
z_{s,c} > 0.
\]

A useful prior is a Gamma distribution:

\begin{mathbox}{Abundance prior}
\begin{align}
\log \mu_{s,c} &= \eta_c + u_{s,c}, \label{eq:meanabundance}\\
z_{s,c} &\sim \Gam\left(r_z,\; \frac{r_z}{\mu_{s,c}}\right). \label{eq:zprior}
\end{align}
\end{mathbox}

This Gamma parameterization uses shape \(r_z\) and rate \(r_z/\mu_{s,c}\), so
\[
\E[z_{s,c}]=\mu_{s,c}.
\]

\subsection{What are \texorpdfstring{\(\eta_c\)}{eta} and \texorpdfstring{\(u_{s,c}\)}{u}?}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
    \item \(\eta_c\) is the global baseline abundance of cell type \(c\).  Common cell types can have larger \(\eta_c\).
    \item \(u_{s,c}\) is a spatial effect.  It lets a cell type be enriched in some tissue regions and depleted in others.
\end{itemize}

A simple prior for \(\eta_c\) is:
\[
\eta_c \sim \Normal(m_{\eta,c},\sigma_{\eta}^{2}).
\]

\subsection{Optional spatial prior}

Neighboring spots often have related cell mixtures.  To encode this, use a conditional autoregressive-like prior:

\begin{mathbox}{Spatial smoothing prior}
\begin{align}
\boldsymbol{u}_{\cdot,c}
&\sim \Normal\left(\boldsymbol{0},\; \tau_c^{-1}(D-\varphi_c W+\epsilon I)^{-1}\right). \label{eq:car}
\end{align}
\end{mathbox}

Here:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
    \item \(W\) is a spot-neighbor graph.  \(W_{s,t}\) is large if spots \(s\) and \(t\) are neighbors that should be smoothed together.
    \item \(D\) is diagonal with \(D_{s,s}=\sum_t W_{s,t}\).
    \item \(\tau_c\) controls how strongly the spatial effect is shrunk toward smoothness.
    \item \(\varphi_c\) controls spatial dependence.  If \(\varphi_c=0\), there is little spatial sharing.  If \(\varphi_c\) is large, nearby spots tend to be similar.
    \item \(\epsilon I\) is a tiny stabilizer that makes the matrix invertible.
\end{itemize}

For a first prototype, this spatial prior can be removed.  Then set \(u_{s,c}=0\), and let each spot be deconvolved independently.

\section{Complete core model}

Putting the pieces together:

\begin{mathbox}{Complete core ShapeMix-ATAC model}
\begin{align}
Y_{s,p,b} &\sim \NB(\lambda_{s,p,b},\phi_p), \\
\lambda_{s,p,b}
&= \ell_s\left[\beta_{p,b} + \sum_{c=1}^{C} z_{s,c}\,q_c\,d_p\,\theta_{c,p}\,\omega_{c,p,b}\right], \\
z_{s,c} &\sim \Gam\left(r_z, \frac{r_z}{\exp(\eta_c+u_{s,c})}\right), \\
\eta_c &\sim \Normal(m_{\eta,c},\sigma_{\eta}^{2}), \\
\boldsymbol{u}_{\cdot,c} &\sim \Normal\left(\boldsymbol{0},\tau_c^{-1}(D-\varphi_c W+\epsilon I)^{-1}\right), \\
\tau_c &\sim \Gam(a_{\tau},b_{\tau}), \\
\varphi_c &\sim \Betadist(a_{\varphi},b_{\varphi}) \quad \text{truncated to } [0,1), \\
q_c &\sim \LogNormal(m_q,\sigma_q^2), \\
d_p &\sim \LogNormal(m_d+\gamma_w \log \mathrm{width}_p+\gamma_{GC}\mathrm{GC}_p,\sigma_d^2), \\
\theta_{c,p} &\sim \Betadist(a_{\theta}+R_{c,p}^{\mathrm{open}},\;b_{\theta}+R_{c,p}^{\mathrm{closed}}), \\
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{c,p,\cdot} &\sim \Dir(a_{\omega}\boldsymbol{u}_{\omega}+\boldsymbol{R}^{\mathrm{shape}}_{c,p,\cdot}), \\
\beta_{p,b} &\sim \Gam(a_{\beta},b_{\beta}), \\
\phi_p &\sim \Gam(a_{\phi},b_{\phi}).
\end{align}
\end{mathbox}

The output cell-type proportion is not a new random variable.  It is computed from the inferred abundances:
\[
\pi_{s,c}=\frac{z_{s,c}}{\sum_{c'=1}^{C} z_{s,c'}}.
\]

\section{Parameter guide: what is a hyperparameter?}

\begin{plainbox}{Rule used in this document}
A \textbf{hyperparameter} is fixed before inference, chosen by the user, cross-validation, weak default settings, or reference-based pseudo-counts.  A \textbf{model parameter} or \textbf{latent variable} is inferred from the data and has a prior distribution.
\end{plainbox}

\(\tau_c\) & spatial precision for cell type \(c\) & No & \(\tau_c\sim\Gam(a_{\tau},b_{\tau})\). \\
\(\varphi_c\) & spatial dependence strength for cell type \(c\) & No & \(\varphi_c\sim\Betadist(a_{\varphi},b_{\varphi})\), truncated to \([0,1)\). \\
\(q_c\) & per-cell ATAC yield for cell type \(c\) & No & \(q_c\sim\LogNormal(m_q,\sigma_q^2)\). \\
\(d_p\) & peak detectability / technical capture factor & No & \(d_p\sim\LogNormal(m_d+\gamma_w\log\mathrm{width}_p+\gamma_{GC}\mathrm{GC}_p,\sigma_d^2)\). \\
\(\gamma_w\) & coefficient for peak width effect on detectability & No & \(\gamma_w\sim\Normal(0,\sigma_{\gamma}^{2})\), or fixed to 0 in the simplest version. \\
\(\gamma_{GC}\) & coefficient for GC effect on detectability & No & \(\gamma_{GC}\sim\Normal(0,\sigma_{\gamma}^{2})\), or fixed to 0 in the simplest version. \\
\(\theta_{c,p}\) & accessibility probability/intensity of peak \(p\) in cell type \(c\) & No & \(\theta_{c,p}\sim\Betadist(a_{\theta}+R_{c,p}^{\mathrm{open}},b_{\theta}+R_{c,p}^{\mathrm{closed}})\). \\
\(\omega_{c,p,b}\) & probability that fragments from \((c,p)\) fall in shape bin \(b\) & No & \(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{c,p,\cdot}\sim\Dir(a_{\omega}\boldsymbol{u}_{\omega}+\boldsymbol{R}^{\mathrm{shape}}_{c,p,\cdot})\). \\
\(\beta_{p,b}\) & background rate for peak \(p\), bin \(b\) & No & \(\beta_{p,b}\sim\Gam(a_{\beta},b_{\beta})\). \\
\(\phi_p\) & negative-binomial dispersion for peak \(p\) & No & \(\phi_p\sim\Gam(a_{\phi},b_{\phi})\). \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

\section{Worked example: why shape helps}

Suppose there are only two cell types: T cells and B cells.  At one peak, both cell types have the same total accessibility, so a peak-count-only model cannot distinguish them.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{L{0.28\linewidth}C{0.2\linewidth}C{0.2\linewidth}C{0.2\linewidth}}
\toprule
Cell type / spot & Total count at peak & Short fragments & Mono fragments \\
\midrule
T-cell reference & 100 & 80\% & 20\% \\
B-cell reference & 100 & 20\% & 80\% \\
Observed spot & 100 & 60\% & 40\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Peak-count model sees only:
\[
100 \approx 100.
\]
It learns almost nothing.

ShapeMix sees:
\[
0.60 \approx 0.80\pi_T+0.20(1-\pi_T).
\]
Solving:
\begin{align*}
0.60 &= 0.80\pi_T+0.20-0.20\pi_T,\\
0.40 &= 0.60\pi_T,\\
\pi_T &\approx 0.67.
\end{align*}

So the observed spot looks like roughly:
\[
\boxed{67\%\;\text{T cell} \quad + \quad 33\%\;\text{B cell}.}
\]

\section{What the posterior means}

The posterior is the full probability distribution over all unknown quantities after seeing the data:

\begin{align}
&p(\{z,\eta,u,\tau,\varphi,q,d,\gamma,\theta,\omega,\beta,\phi\}\given Y) \\
&\quad \propto
\prod_{s,p,b} p(Y_{s,p,b}\given \lambda_{s,p,b},\phi_p)
\prod_{s,c} p(z_{s,c}\given \eta_c,u_{s,c})
\prod_c p(\eta_c)p(\boldsymbol{u}_{\cdot,c}\given \tau_c,\varphi_c)p(\tau_c)p(\varphi_c) \\
&\qquad \times
\prod_c p(q_c)
\prod_p p(d_p)
\prod_{c,p} p(\theta_{c,p})p(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{c,p,\cdot})
\prod_{p,b} p(\beta_{p,b})
\prod_p p(\phi_p).
\end{align}

The model output should report not only one best estimate, but also uncertainty:
\[
\widehat{\pi}_{s,c}=\E[\pi_{s,c}\given Y],
\qquad
95\%\;\text{credible interval for }\pi_{s,c}.
\]

\begin{plainbox}{Why uncertainty matters}
If a rare cell type is predicted at 2\%, but the 95\% credible interval is 0\% to 12\%, the model is not confident.  This is better than pretending the 2\% estimate is exact.
\end{plainbox}

\section{Fitting the model}

There are two practical inference choices.

\subsection{Option 1: MAP estimation}

MAP means maximum a posteriori.  It finds the single parameter setting that maximizes:
\[
\log p(Y\given \Theta)+\log p(\Theta),
\]
where \(\Theta\) means all unknown quantities.

This is easier to implement and debug.  It is a good first prototype.

\subsection{Option 2: variational inference}

Variational inference approximates the posterior with a simpler distribution:
\[
q(\Theta) \approx p(\Theta\given Y).
\]

Then it optimizes an evidence lower bound, usually called the ELBO:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}
= \E_q[\log p(Y,\Theta)] - \E_q[\log q(\Theta)].
\]

This gives uncertainty estimates and scales better than MCMC for many spots and peaks.

\begin{plainbox}{Recommended development path}
Start with MAP for the minimal model.  Once it works, implement variational inference for uncertainty and scalability.
\end{plainbox}

\section{Minimal prototype}

The full model is useful for a paper, but the first code version should be simpler.

\subsection{Minimal model equations}

Fix \(\theta_{c,p}\), \(\omega_{c,p,b}\), \(d_p\), \(q_c\), and \(\phi_p\) from reference or empirical estimates.  Infer only \(z_{s,c}\) and maybe \(\beta_{p,b}\):

\begin{align}
Y_{s,p,b} &\sim \NB(\lambda_{s,p,b},\phi_p),\\
\lambda_{s,p,b} &= \ell_s\left[\beta_{p,b}+\sum_c z_{s,c} q_c d_p\theta_{c,p}\omega_{c,p,b}\right],\\
z_{s,c} &\sim \Gam(r_z,r_z/m_c),\\
\beta_{p,b} &\sim \Gam(a_\beta,b_\beta).
\end{align}

\section{How to build the shape-count tensor}

\subsection{Step-by-step preprocessing}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
    \item Start with an ATAC fragment file containing chromosome, start, end, and spatial barcode.
    \item Intersect each fragment with a chosen peak set.
    \item Assign each fragment to a shape bin.
    \item Count fragments by spot, peak, and shape bin.
    \item Keep informative peaks or peak groups.
    \item Build the tensor \(Y_{s,p,b}\).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Fragment-length bin example}

For fragment length \(L\):
\[
 b(L)=
 \begin{cases}
 1, & L < 100 \quad \text{short},\\
 2, & 100 \le L < 250 \quad \text{mono-nucleosomal-like},\\
 3, & L \ge 250 \quad \text{long}.
 \end{cases}
\]

The exact cutoffs should be tuned to the assay and species.

\section{Diagnostics and ablation tests}

\subsection{Diagnostics}

After fitting, check:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
    \item \textbf{Posterior predictive checks:} simulate \(Y^{\mathrm{rep}}\) from the fitted model and compare with observed \(Y\).
    \item \textbf{Shape-bin reconstruction:} does the model reproduce short/mono/long ratios?
    \item \textbf{Rare-cell uncertainty:} rare cell types should have wider intervals unless evidence is strong.
    \item \textbf{Reference mismatch:} large background \(\beta_{p,b}\) or systematic residuals suggest missing cell states.
    \item \textbf{Spatial smoothness:} if using the spatial prior, check that it smooths within tissue domains but does not erase boundaries.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Ablation tests}

Ablation means removing one ingredient at a time to test whether it matters.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{L{0.28\linewidth}X}
\toprule
Model version & Question answered \\
\midrule
Peak-count only & Does shape add value beyond total accessibility? \\
ShapeMix with length bins & Does fragment length improve deconvolution? \\
ShapeMix with position bins & Does within-peak position improve deconvolution? \\
No spatial prior & Does spatial smoothing help? \\
No peak detectability \(d_p\) & Are peak-specific technical effects important? \\
No background \(\beta_{p,b}\) & Is unexplained signal being forced into wrong cell types? \\
Fixed signatures vs Bayesian signatures & Does uncertainty in the reference matter? \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\section{What makes ShapeMix-ATAC new and testable?}

The standard transferred deconvolution methods use spot-by-feature count matrices.  For ATAC, the feature is usually a peak.  ShapeMix changes the data object from a matrix to a tensor:
\[
\text{ordinary ATAC deconvolution: } Y_{s,p}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\text{ShapeMix-ATAC: }Y_{s,p,b}.
\]

\begin{idea}
ShapeMix-ATAC tests the hypothesis that fragment-shape information helps distinguish cell types whose total accessibility profiles are too similar.
\end{idea}

This is especially useful for:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
    \item closely related immune subtypes,
    \item nearby developmental states,
    \item tumor and stromal mixtures,
    \item rare cell types with subtle peak-count differences,
    \item cell types that share open peaks but differ in nucleosome or footprint patterns.
\end{itemize}

\section{Implementation pseudocode}

\begin{plainbox}{Pseudocode}
\begin{verbatim}
Input:
    spatial ATAC fragments
    peaks or peak groups
    scATAC/multiome reference with cell type labels
    spatial coordinates

Preprocess:
    assign each spatial fragment to a peak p
    assign each fragment to a shape bin b
    build tensor Y[s,p,b]
    build reference signatures theta[c,p] and omega[c,p,b]

Fit minimal model:
    initialize z[s,c] using peak-count deconvolution or NNLS
    repeat until convergence:
        compute lambda[s,p,b]
        update z[s,c] to improve NB likelihood plus prior
        optionally update beta[p,b]

Fit full model:
    add priors for theta, omega, q, d, phi
    add spatial prior for u[s,c]
    use variational inference or MAP optimization

Output:
    pi[s,c] = z[s,c] / sum_c z[s,c]
    uncertainty intervals
    posterior predictive checks
    residual peaks and residual shape patterns
\end{verbatim}
\end{plainbox}

\section{Common questions}

\subsection{Is \texorpdfstring{\(\omega_{c,p,b}\)}{omega} a hyperparameter?}

No.  In the full Bayesian model, \(\omega_{c,p,b}\) is an inferred model parameter with a Dirichlet prior.  In the minimal prototype, it can be fixed from the reference.  If fixed, it is not a hyperparameter in the usual Bayesian sense; it is an empirical input.

\subsection{Is \texorpdfstring{\(\theta_{c,p}\)}{theta} a hyperparameter?}

No.  In the full model, \(\theta_{c,p}\) is an inferred model parameter with a Beta prior.  The hyperparameters are \(a_\theta\), \(b_\theta\), and the reference pseudo-count construction.

\subsection{Is \texorpdfstring{\(z_{s,c}\)}{z} a cell count?}

It is an \emph{effective} cell count.  It behaves like a positive abundance.  It may not be an exact integer because spatial ATAC counts depend on capture efficiency, chromatin openness, and sequencing depth.  If image-derived nuclei counts are available, the model can constrain \(\sum_c z_{s,c}\) to match them.

\subsection{Can we use Poisson instead of negative binomial?}

Yes.  The Poisson version is:
\[
Y_{s,p,b}\sim\Pois(\lambda_{s,p,b}).
\]
It is simpler and may work well for some ATAC peak counts.  The negative binomial is safer when the data are overdispersed.

\subsection{Do we need all peaks?}

No.  Use selected peaks or peak groups.  ShapeMix is most useful when the selected peaks have informative shape differences between cell types.  A practical first version might use 5,000 to 30,000 peaks or groups, depending on depth.

\section{Optional extension: image-based cell budget}

If nuclei segmentation gives an approximate number of nuclei \(N_s^{\mathrm{img}}\) in spot \(s\), add:
\[
N_s^{\mathrm{img}} \sim \Normal_+\left(\sum_c z_{s,c},\sigma_N^2\right),
\]
where \(\Normal_+\) means a Normal distribution truncated to positive values.

\section{Optional extension: peak reliability weights}

Some peaks are noisy, poorly mappable, or not useful for deconvolution.  Add a reliability weight \(r_p\):
\[
r_p \sim \Betadist(a_r,b_r),
\qquad 0<r_p<1.
\]

Then use a weighted log-likelihood:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{weighted}}
=\sum_{s,p,b} r_p\log p(Y_{s,p,b}\given\lambda_{s,p,b},\phi_p).
\]

Parameter status:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{L{0.18\linewidth}C{0.15\linewidth}X}
\toprule
Symbol & Hyper? & Distribution / status \\
\midrule
\(r_p\) & No & \(r_p\sim\Betadist(a_r,b_r)\). \\
\(a_r,b_r\) & Yes & Fixed Beta prior parameters. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

This extension is useful, but it is not required for the first ShapeMix paper.  The core novelty is already the shape-count likelihood.

\section{Recommended first experiment}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
    \item Build a peak-count baseline using \(Y_{s,p}=\sum_b Y_{s,p,b}\).
    \item Build ShapeMix with two or three fragment-length bins.
    \item Simulate pseudo-spots from a scATAC or multiome reference.
    \item Compare true and estimated \(\pi_{s,c}\).
    \item Stress-test related cell types and rare cell types.
    \item Report whether shape improves RMSE, Jensen-Shannon divergence, rare-cell F1, and calibration.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{warningbox}
Do not begin with too many shape bins.  If \(P\) peaks and \(B\) bins are both large, the tensor becomes sparse.  Start simple, prove shape helps, then add richer shape features.
\end{warningbox}

\section{Summary in one page}

\begin{mathbox}{The whole model in one line}
\[
Y_{s,p,b}\sim\NB\left(
\ell_s\left[\beta_{p,b}+\sum_c z_{s,c}q_c d_p\theta_{c,p}\omega_{c,p,b}\right],\phi_p
\right).
\]
\end{mathbox}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
    \item \(Y_{s,p,b}\): observed shape-count tensor.
    \item \(z_{s,c}\): hidden amount of cell type \(c\) in spot \(s\).
    \item \(\theta_{c,p}\): how open peak \(p\) is in cell type \(c\).
    \item \(\omega_{c,p,b}\): what shape fragments from cell type \(c\) have at peak \(p\).
    \item \(d_p\): peak detectability.
    \item \(q_c\): cell-type ATAC yield.
    \item \(\beta_{p,b}\): background.
    \item \(\phi_p\): overdispersion.
\end{itemize}

The final cell-type composition is:
\[
\pi_{s,c}=\frac{z_{s,c}}{\sum_{c'}z_{s,c'}}.
\]

\begin{idea}
ShapeMix-ATAC is easy to explain because it says: \emph{use the pattern of fragments, not only the number of fragments}.  It is intuitive because ATAC fragments physically reflect chromatin structure.  It is testable because you can compare the same model with and without the shape-bin index \(b\).
\end{idea}
